How It Works

How a 43,500-member House would actually govern

One principle runs through everything on this page.

Power pools everywhere. So every place power can pool gets a drain that the members themselves control.

The members can route around leadership. A coalition can route around the agenda gate. The full floor can route around any panel or administrator. Nothing below is a separate clever trick; each is this one idea applied at a different altitude. If a mechanism ever fails, the drain beneath it is the design working — not the design breaking.

Three layers: what’s fixed, what adapts, what merely serves.

What follows is one workable design — proof that workable designs exist — not the only one. Three layers keep that honest. The constitutional layer guarantees only what must never be capturable: community-scale districts, a House that grows with the population, every member’s equal public vote, and the drains themselves — the members’ structural power to force consideration past any gatekeeper. The foundational rulebook holds the specific numbers and definitions, changeable only by a broad supermajority of members — adjustable, but never quietly. And ordinary rules and technology — committee organization, schedules, the voting and scoring systems — stay fully adaptable: tools that serve the institution and never control it.

Fixed where capture is possible. Flexible everywhere else. Experience gets to improve this design without anyone being able to steal it.

SCALE

Drawn as communities. Worked from communities.

Where would 43,500 representatives actually work? Mostly where they already live.

Districts are drawn to equal population along recognizable community boundaries — at 7,600 people, the lines can follow neighborhoods and towns instead of slicing through them. (Binding fair-districting rules are still required; small lines are easier to justify, not automatically honest.)

Members stay based in the districts that elected them. Committee work and voting happen remotely; a few coordinating functions run in person. For most of history, legislating required gathering in one building — that constraint is simply gone, and removing it is what keeps a representative living with the same schools, roads, and local economy as the people they answer to.

A working day, district edition: a constituent meeting at nine. A committee briefing at eleven. Legislation to review in the afternoon, a community meeting that evening. The phone rings — not a donor; someone whose door you knocked on.

What it changes → MONEY

A real salary, a small office, a campaign simple enough to run with volunteers.

What does the job pay — and who can afford to take it?

A real, full-time salary. This is the one place the design deliberately does not copy New Hampshire: its $100-a-year model quietly restricts service to people who can afford to work free. A national House must be affordable to hold, not just to win — the seat has to fit a nurse’s finances, not just her reputation. And the total cost stays small: even 43,500 full salaries amount to a fraction of one percent of federal spending.

Offices stay small — a district staffer or two — while deep policy expertise pools at the committee level instead of being duplicated across 43,500 private policy shops. And the rules governing a community-scale campaign are kept simple enough that ten volunteers can comply without a compliance lawyer: the legal overhead of running has to shrink with the district, or the door only looks open.

What it changes → POWER

Three lanes to the floor — and no gatekeeper who can bury a bill.

Who decides what gets a vote? In today’s House, a handful of leaders. Here: no one — and everyone.

The chokepoint isn’t staffed with better people; it’s removed. A bill reaches the floor through any of three lanes.

It bridges. A bill advances when it draws support from across caucuses — scored by a published algorithm running on members’ public signals. Support from other factions is what moves it, so a disciplined bloc mass-backing its own bill gets nowhere. This is the normal route, and it filters out divisive squeakers before they reach a vote.

It’s pushed. A large coalition of members — a fixed percentage written in the rulebook — can force any bill to the floor with no bridging required. This is the safety valve for bold, contested reform: the lane that keeps a consensus system from smothering exactly the kind of transformative idea this site proposes.

It’s mandatory. Appropriations, reauthorizations, and defined oversight advance on a fixed calendar, whether or not anyone finds them exciting. The unglamorous business of government never dies in a feed.

Three rules keep the lanes honest. Signals come from members only — one member, one non-transferable signal, every one public. Signals advance bills; they can never bury one — killing a bill takes a recorded floor vote. And where judgment is irreducible — is this bill genuinely single-subject? genuinely mandatory? — it goes to a sortition panel: a few hundred members drawn by lot for that bill alone, deciding with published reasons. A standing committee can be lobbied for years; a panel that didn’t exist last week and won’t exist next week cannot be cultivated. Above all of it sits the override: a supermajority coalition can force a vote past any panel, any administrator, any rule-keeper. No one has to be trusted, because everyone can be routed around.

Two real-world lessons are baked in. Germany’s Pirate Party let members delegate votes — delegations chained, piled up, and manufactured “super-voters” whose word moved blocs; so here, delegation doesn’t exist, in any form. And Taiwan’s vTaiwan showed that ranking by cross-group agreement surfaces what broad publics actually share, where ranking by raw popularity rewards whoever shouts loudest — its bridging principle carries over, with the honest caveat that vTaiwan informed legislation rather than running a chamber.

What it changes → FUNCTION

Committees draft. The floor decides. No one drinks the firehose.

A modern two-year Congress sees 15,000 or more bills introduced. Who reads them all? No one — and no one has to.

Every member specializes: a handful of committees, chosen by the member, with a lottery backstop so no domain ever sits empty. Inside their lane, members do the detailed work — hearings, drafting, markup — and cast genuinely informed votes. A body of 43,500 can staff real experts in every domain the government touches; the water-rights person and the pediatric-drug-pricing person can actually exist.

Outside their lane, members can follow advisory recommendations published by committees or colleagues they trust. Advisory means exactly this: a recommendation never casts a vote. The member clicks, under their own name, on their own record, and can ignore any recommendation on any bill. Recommendations are domain-scoped and public; there are no follower counts and no leaderboards to manufacture celebrity signalers. Handing your vote to someone else — delegation — does not exist anywhere in the system.

Delegation (banned)Advisory signal (how it works here)
Casts your vote for youRecommends only — you cast every vote, under your name
Chains and accumulates into super-votersNever chains — one member, one non-transferable signal
Sits unrevoked for yearsRevocable on any bill, instantly
OpaquePublic and domain-scoped; no follower leaderboards

The floor itself is a voting venue, not a debating hall. Final votes are cast electronically from the districts, public and attributable. Deliberation lives in committees; coordination lives in caucuses that form and re-form issue by issue.

What it changes → RULES

Fixed numbers, open math, public votes.

What keeps this machinery honest? Nothing you have to trust.

The thresholds are fixed numbers published in the foundational rulebook, changeable only by a broad supermajority — dials bolted down, not turned monthly by a committee. The bridging score is open-source math computed on public votes: a number anyone can recompute, never a verdict someone renders. The instant a human “interprets” the result, that human is a chokepoint — so no human does.

Bills carry a single-subject rule, already common in state constitutions: a proposal does one nameable thing and reads that way. And everything — every vote, every signal, every override — is public, attributable, tamper-evident, and independently checkable. Sunlight isn’t a courtesy in this design. It’s the enforcement mechanism.

What it changes →

What’s honestly unresolved.

Three things, named plainly.

The exact numbers. The coalition-lane threshold lives in a Goldilocks band: set it too high and almost nothing moves; too low and the floor floods — and a flooded floor would need triage, which is a gatekeeper sneaking back in. The right settings must be found by stress-testing, then locked into the rulebook. That work is real, and it is undone.

The keystone assumption. Specialization only works if members actually do their committee homework rather than rubber-stamping a caucus signal across every domain. The design buys them the time — no fundraising treadmill — but no structure can force diligence. This is the assumption the whole machine leans on hardest.

The quiet corners. Domains nobody watches draw the least scrutiny — true of every institution, and true here. Transparency plus a cheaply contestable seat is the realistic mitigation, not a guarantee.

None of these is hidden, and none is fatal. The standard this design holds itself to is comparative, not absolute: wherever it stays imperfect, the imperfection is smaller, more visible, and easier for ordinary members to correct than its equivalent in the House we have now.

That’s the machine.

Communities drawn honestly, a job ordinary people can afford to hold, three lanes no gatekeeper controls, committees that draft and a floor that decides, and rules that ask for audit instead of trust. What all of it buys — in your district, your state, and the country — is next.
