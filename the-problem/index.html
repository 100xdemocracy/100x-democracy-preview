The Problem

Why does Congress keep failing no matter whom voters elect?

86% of Americans disapprove of Congress. 97% of incumbents win anyway.

In April 2026, Gallup measured congressional approval at 10 percent — tying the lowest number it has ever recorded. Months earlier, in the 2024 elections, about 97 percent of House incumbents who ran for reelection won.

Those two numbers should not be able to coexist. No product that 86 percent of customers reject keeps a 97 percent renewal rate — unless the customers have nowhere else to go. That is the actual situation: the ballot offers two national brands, and for most voters, punishing the incumbent means hiring the brand they dislike even more. So the anger has nowhere to go, and it goes nowhere. Election after election.

This page is about why that is. The short answer: it is not mainly the voters, and it is not even mainly the politicians. It is the structure they are both trapped in.

One cause, five symptoms

A century ago, Congress made a quiet decision: the House of Representatives stopped growing. The country didn’t. That single fact set off a chain reaction. Districts became enormous, so representation became distant. Reaching a district that size became industrially expensive, so candidates became dependent on money and institutions. National power compressed into 435 seats, so those seats became worth capturing. The chamber hardened into two blocs that can barely decide anything. And the process that runs it turned opaque enough that only insiders can follow it.

Distance. Price. Concentration. Paralysis. Opacity. Five different complaints — and one structural cause. Each one is traced below, and each can be followed as its own thread through this entire site: the problem here, the answer on the next page, the hard questions after that, the machinery after that.

SCALE

The House stopped growing. The districts never did.

How many people does your representative represent? About 760,000 — more than the entire population of Seattle.

For the country’s first 120 years, the House grew with the population, census after census. In 1910, each member represented roughly 200,000 people. Then, in 1929, Congress froze the chamber at 435 seats — and left it there. The population has roughly tripled since. The seat count has not moved.

Today the average district holds about 760,000 people. Nobody chose that distance. No one voted to make representation nearly four times more remote. It simply accumulated, decade after decade, because the divisor was frozen while the dividend kept growing.

The capacity to respond was frozen too: since the 1970s, each member’s office has been capped at 18 permanent staff — while every district added hundreds of thousands of constituents. When you call your representative and can’t get through, that isn’t laziness. It’s arithmetic.

This is not just our diagnosis. Vox told the story of the freeze this year — how the House stopped growing, what that did to representation, and the live bill in Congress from Rep. Sean Casten that would begin reversing it. Scholars, journalists, and a sitting congressman agree the House is far too small; their proposals stop at more modest sizes than ours, and the difference matters — but on the diagnosis, there is no longer much argument.

Among democracies, America is an extreme outlier. The United Kingdom has roughly one member of Parliament for every 100,000 people; Germany, one for every 130,000. Only India — with 1.4 billion people — packs more constituents into a lower-house district than the United States does; Japan’s districts hold about a third as many. And America already contains the counterexample: New Hampshire’s state house runs on districts of about 3,300 people. It has for generations.

And here is the part almost no one knows: this failure was nearly prevented at the founding. The very first constitutional amendment ever proposed — written by James Madison, listed first in the package that became the Bill of Rights — was designed to force the House to keep growing with the population. By the end of 1791 it stood one state short of ratification. It has never been adopted. The distance you live with today is the road that was taken instead.

How 100× answers this → MONEY

The price of entry: about $2.3 million and fifteen months of your life.

What does it actually take to run for Congress today? Start with the size of the audience: 760,000 people, almost none of whom know your name.

You cannot knock on 760,000 doors. So reaching a district that size means buying reach — staff, polling, mail, television, digital, video, compliance, and the consultants who operate it all. For a competitive seat, that machine costs on the order of $2.3 million. And it starts running about fifteen months before Election Day, because you are not running one race but two: months of campaigning to win a party’s nomination, then months more to win the general.

Fundraising at that scale is not a task; it is the job. Two-point-three million dollars over fifteen months is roughly $5,000 raised every single day. Mornings go to donor calls. Evenings go to fundraisers. The candidate’s actual ideas get whatever is left.

You call someone connected to the party to say you’re thinking about running. They ask three questions: How much have you raised? Which endorsements do you have? Who’s your campaign manager? — and then: Call me when you have real money and a seasoned team.

Now look at who can pay that price. Not the person your community already trusts — the nurse, the electrician, the teacher, the small-business owner. They look at the number, and they do the sane thing: they stay home. The door isn’t locked because they’d be bad at the job. It’s locked by the price of reaching three-quarters of a million strangers. Meanwhile, the incumbent starts every cycle with the donor file, the endorsements, and the consultants from the last three races already in place.

How 100× answers this → POWER

435 seats make power cheap to capture and hard to correct.

How much of Congress do you need to influence to influence Congress? Far less than you’d think.

Compress the legislative power of 340 million people into 435 seats, and each seat becomes extraordinarily valuable — and the whole system becomes cheap to steer. Concentrated power fails in three ways at once. It is cheaper to influence, because only a few people matter. It is more damaging when abused, because one well-placed actor can move the entire system. And it is harder to correct, because the people holding it write the rules for challenging them.

Inside the chamber, the concentration has a specific shape: the calendar. Most bills do not die by losing a vote. They die by never getting one — because leadership controls what reaches the floor, and what never does. Add the money: members depend on leadership-blessed fundraising to survive districts this expensive, so committee seats and campaign funds become instruments of discipline. Careers are owned. Votes follow.

That 97 percent reelection rate isn’t a satisfaction score. It is the price tag working as designed: a seat of 760,000 people costs so much to contest that almost no one credible tries. A vote you cannot lose is a vote you never have to earn.

How 100× answers this → FUNCTION

A chamber of 435 professionals that can barely decide anything.

If concentrated power at least worked — if the House were decisive — there would be a trade to weigh. It isn’t, and there isn’t.

Today’s House runs as two disciplined blocs that vote against each other on command. Whipping 435 people into two rigid teams is entirely feasible — that’s few enough careers for leadership to control — and once both teams hold, 218 votes can stonewall anything. The gridlock everyone hates is not caused by too many voices. It is caused by too few independent ones.

Scarcity does the rest. Floor time is so limited that legislation piles into rare, must-pass omnibus vehicles — thousand-page bills assembled by a handful of offices and voted on largely unread, because everyone knows there won’t be another train leaving the station. Bills with broad public support die quietly, unscheduled, year after year. The public watches and concludes the members must be lazy or corrupt. Mostly, they are neither. They are inside a machine built to stall.

How 100× answers this → RULES

The process is unreadable — and that’s not an accident.

Could you explain how a bill actually becomes law? Not the schoolhouse version — the real one?

Almost no one can. How an omnibus actually gets assembled, and by whom. How the Rules Committee decides what may even be amended on the floor. How a measure most Americans support dies without a recorded vote, so there is no one to blame. The real process runs on procedures that only its operators understand.

That opacity is not a side effect of hard problems. Policy is genuinely complicated; the democratic rules — who may propose, how things reach a vote, who is accountable for the result — do not have to be. When they are tangled anyway, the tangle works like an asset: a rule you cannot understand is a rule you cannot check, and a process no one can audit belongs to the people who operate it, not to the public. Legitimacy leaks out of a system nobody can verify. That is where trust in Congress went.

How 100× answers this →

Different symptoms. One structural cause.

Distance, price, concentration, paralysis, opacity. Five complaints that sound like separate diseases — and trace back to a single decision: too many people, represented through too few seats. The House was frozen while the country tripled, and everything on this page follows from that.

Which is also the good news. Problems with one structural cause can have one structural fix.
