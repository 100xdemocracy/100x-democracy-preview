Impact

What actually changes for you, your community, and your state

Representation becomes something you can use — not just something you watch.

The last two pages explained the mechanisms. This one is the consequences, at three altitudes: your district, your state, the country. Everything below follows from one change — a district of about 7,600 people instead of 760,000 — and none of it requires anyone in power to behave better. It requires the seat to be close enough to reach and small enough to contest.

Your district: the seat comes within reach.

Start at your own front door. Your district shrinks to a few neighborhoods — and the office changes character completely.

What does 7,600 people look like? In a city: a few blocks — the district fits inside a short walk. In the suburbs: a couple of subdivisions and the school between them. In the country: a small town and the roads around it — more land, same knowability. In every version, the district is a place you already navigate, not a media market.

SCALEYou can reach the seat.

Your representative can be a visible part of the community instead of a distant national figure serving hundreds of thousands. A meeting, a public forum, a neighborhood group, an organized call from constituents — these become hard to ignore. And a problem affecting one town, one workplace, one school no longer disappears inside a district of 760,000 people. It’s a meaningful share of the whole district.

MONEYYou can shape who holds it.

Participation stops meaning choosing between two finished options and starts meaning helping create the options. Neighbors can recruit a respected local person to run before money or party institutions decide who counts as viable. Direct contact, volunteers, and reputation can compete with fundraising. When a representative stops listening, organizing a credible challenge becomes realistic — and running yourself becomes imaginable for far more people than career politicians and the independently wealthy.

POWERYour community can become a real political force.

In a district of 7,600, a few hundred organized neighbors are no longer a rounding error — they are a base that can elect, pressure, or replace a representative. The sequence is short and real: a local problem appears; residents can name exactly who’s affected; neighbors organize and make their position visible; the seat must respond, because the pressure is coming from a meaningful share of the district; and if it doesn’t respond, the community can escalate — recruit a challenger, build a campaign, replace the person in office. The seat becomes a source of power the community can actually use.

RULESAnd you can check all of it. Every vote your representative casts is public and attributable, every path a bill takes is traceable, and the rules that govern it are short enough to read. You don’t have to take anyone’s word for whether the system is working — you can look.

Your state: a delegation too big to buy.

What happens to your state’s voice in the House?

It multiplies. Every state’s delegation grows roughly a hundredfold — a state with 8 seats today holds on the order of 800; California’s delegation numbers in the thousands. No state loses seats. What shifts, slightly, is relative share: at 43,500 seats, representation tracks population almost exactly, so the rounding luck that currently gives some small states a little extra weight — and shorts others — disappears. How the numbers are calculated

The official 2020 Census apportionment population (331,108,434) — the same population basis used to divide today’s 435 seats — allocated across 43,500 seats using the Huntington-Hill method of equal proportions, the same formula the House uses today. Each state is guaranteed at least one seat. At this scale, alternative standard apportionment methods differ by only a handful of seats. Relative weight change = (state’s share of 43,500 seats ÷ state’s share of 435 seats) − 1. No state loses seats; every delegation grows roughly a hundredfold.

POWERThe deeper change isn’t the count — it’s what the count does to leverage. A delegation of thousands can’t be whipped as a unit, bought as a unit, or traded as a unit. Your state’s voice in the House stops being a handful of careers that leadership can manage and becomes something closer to the state itself: too many independent members, answering to too many separate communities, to be anyone’s lottery ticket.

The country: a Congress ordinary people can enter.

Now zoom all the way out, and run the district-level change everywhere at once.

MONEYThe price of entry collapses in every district simultaneously. Seats that today go uncontested for lack of anyone able to raise millions become winnable by door-knocking — which means far more of the country’s races become realistically contestable, cycle after cycle. Incumbency stops being a fortress and goes back to being a record to defend.

POWERNo single seat is worth buying. Influence that once needed to reach a few pivotal careers now has to persuade thousands of independent members, each answerable to a community that knows them. Concentrated money doesn’t vanish from politics — it loses its cheapest targets.

FUNCTIONLegislation changes shape. With abundant paths to the floor, bills no longer pile into thousand-page omnibus vehicles as the only train leaving the station — more measures get their own vote, on their own merits, in a form short enough to read. More gets decided, in the open, by shifting coalitions instead of two locked armies.

And the people in the seats change. Picture it concretely: a nurse who has watched the same problems go unaddressed for twenty years. An electrician who understands how the local economy actually works. A teacher who sees every week what families are struggling with. A warehouse supervisor, a caregiver, a small-business owner — people without fortunes or donor networks, whose communities know exactly who they are. Make it real.

None of this happens on its own. Districts this small require a constitutional amendment, and amendments require a movement — one that starts with people deciding this option should exist. There is a real path, and it’s mapped on the next page.
