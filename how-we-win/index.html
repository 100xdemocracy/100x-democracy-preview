How We Win

How an idea this large becomes law

The bar, owned: this requires a constitutional amendment.

Start with the hardest fact, because everything else on this page is built on top of it.

The Constitution bars districts smaller than 30,000 people — which means a district of 7,600 cannot be created by any ordinary act of Congress. A 43,500-member House requires amending the Constitution. There is no shortcut, and this site will never pretend otherwise.

That bar is appropriate. A change this fundamental to how the country is governed should require broad, deliberate national agreement; needing an amendment isn’t a defect in the idea — it reflects the seriousness of it. And the obstacle should be named just as plainly: the amendment must move through a political system whose current officeholders benefit from the scarcity of seats and the concentration of power it would end. That is the real fight. It is political, not architectural, and nothing about the design’s elegance makes it easier.

It is also not a new fight. The first constitutional amendment ever proposed — written by Madison, listed first in the package that became the Bill of Rights — was written to force the House to keep growing with the population. By the end of 1791 it stood one state short of ratification; it remains, technically, pending. The founders believed a growth guarantee belonged in the Constitution. This project is finishing an argument that began in 1789 — and going further than even they proposed, and saying so.

Two legal routes — and the precedent that makes the first one real.

Article V gives exactly two ways to propose an amendment. This strategy uses one and holds the other as leverage.

Plan A: Congress proposes, under pressure it cannot ignore.

Two-thirds of both chambers propose the amendment; thirty-eight states ratify it. The obvious objection is the honest one: why would members ever propose an amendment that dissolves their own seats? Because it has happened before, under exactly one condition. The 17th Amendment — direct election of senators — was proposed in 1912 by the very Senate it disempowered, after decades of public campaigning and roughly thirty state applications had pushed the country to the brink of an Article V convention on the question. Senators didn’t vote to diminish themselves out of virtue. They did it when refusing became costlier than proposing. That is the model: build state-level pressure until proposing the amendment is the safer vote.

Plan B, held as leverage: the Article V convention.

Thirty-four state applications force Congress to call a proposing convention. This strategy treats that route as pressure, not preference, for a reason stated honestly: a convention has no settled rules confining its scope, and the risk of one that wanders is taken seriously across the political spectrum. But the threat is what moved the Senate in 1912, and the threat only works if the applications are real. States that pass applications are not bluffing — they are loading the mechanism that makes Plan A move.

Either route ends in the same place: thirty-eight states. Which is why this campaign lives in the states, not in Washington.

Sources & calibration
  • Article V: amendments are proposed by two-thirds of both chambers of Congress, or by a convention called on the application of two-thirds (34) of the states; ratification requires three-quarters (38) of the states — U.S. Const. Art. V. Supports: The legal path; why states are the terrain.
  • The 17th Amendment (direct election of senators) was proposed in 1912 by the very Senate it disempowered, after decades of public pressure and roughly thirty state applications pushing toward an Article V convention on the question — Senate historical record; Article V application history. Supports: The load-bearing precedent: Congress has proposed an amendment diminishing its own members' power when credible convention pressure made refusing costlier than proposing. The political sequence.

Believable is the standard — not inevitable. “It’s needed” and “it will happen” are different sentences, and this page only gets to say the first one.

One more honest premise before the steps: broken systems reliably produce anger, but anger produces structural reform only when a legible option exists for it to attach to. Historically the menu has been empty — which is why anger curdles into apathy or lurches between the two parties. The first job is therefore not persuasion but supply: put the missing option on the menu, clearly enough that anger has somewhere constructive to go.

  1. Make the idea legible. This site, the video series, and the full written case — the argument in every format a curious person might meet it. (This step is underway; you’re inside it.)
  2. Position it as the missing option. Not another critique of Congress — the structural fix that was never offered. The 86/97 paradox is the hook; community-scale representation is the option.
  3. Build the validator bench. Academics, former officials, reform organizations, and local leaders across the spectrum. The incrementalists — the uncap movement, the 585 proposals, the live expansion bill in Congress — are allies on diagnosis and direction, and should be treated that way: any uncapping of the House is welcome proof of direction, while this project holds its own line that only community scale changes the relationship.
  4. Pass state memorial resolutions. Nonbinding resolutions calling on Congress to propose the amendment — cheap to pass, real as signal, and the training ground for the coalition that later delivers ratification.
  5. Make New Hampshire the first domino. The one state that already lives community-scale representation. A resolution from the country’s 400-member citizen chamber carries a message no other state can send: the people who already govern this way, calling for it nationally.
  6. Hit public milestones. A visible endorser count. The first congressional sponsors — members willing to co-sponsor the amendment that ends their own seat’s scarcity value.
  7. The amendment is introduced — with the state resolutions, the bench, and the count behind it, so it arrives as a movement’s demand rather than a curiosity.
  8. The ratification map. Thirty-eight states, sequenced from the willing to the winnable — with the small-state question addressed head-on (next section) rather than discovered late.

The transition — and the small-state question, named early.

Two things the endgame has to plan for, not stumble into.

The rollout. Nobody flips from 435 to 43,500 overnight. The amendment itself would set a phased schedule — growth in steps across multiple election cycles, with districts, election administration, and House rules maturing alongside. That is a sequencing challenge with a decade-plus horizon, and it is exactly as solvable as it sounds; the difficulty of the road is not an argument against the destination.

The small-state question. Electoral votes are House seats plus Senate seats — so at 43,500, the Electoral College becomes nearly proportional to population, and a small state’s relative presidential weight falls (Wyoming’s share of electoral votes drops to roughly a third of today’s). Thirty-eight states must ratify, and some of them are exactly these states. The honest pitch to them is a trade, made openly: what a small state gives up in presidential rounding advantage, it gains back in actual representation — Wyoming goes from one House member to roughly seventy-six, from one overwhelmed generalist to a real delegation with committee presence across every domain that touches the state. Whether that trade persuades enough small states is a genuinely open strategic question — named here, at the start, because a ratification strategy that hides it would deserve to fail.

What you can do now.

A ladder, not a leap — every rung matters at this stage.

Understand it, then hand it to one person. Read the case; send the page or the video that convinced you to one person who’s angry at Congress and has nowhere to put it.

Endorse the principles. Add your name to the public count. Milestone six is built out of this rung.

Contact your state legislator. Not Congress — your state house. State legislators answer to districts small enough that your message actually lands, and state resolutions are the pressure mechanism this whole strategy runs on. Ask where they stand on a memorial resolution.

Organize locally. Host one discussion — a living room, a library room, a union hall. The skills that organize a neighborhood conversation are the same skills that will someday win a 7,600-person district.

Volunteer what you know. Lawyers, historians, designers, organizers, teachers, apportionment nerds: the movement is being built from exactly the kind of people the proposal would put in Congress.

The road is long, and no one should pretend otherwise. But it has been walked before — by the movement that made senators answer to voters, one state resolution at a time. The destination this time is a Congress ordinary people can enter. The first step is deciding that option should exist.
